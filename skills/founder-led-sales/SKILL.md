# Founder-led sales

## Purpose

Nobody else can sell the product before the founder can. The first deals teach the founder what the product is really for and who will pay for it. Hiring a seller before that point hires someone to do a job that is not yet defined. This skill sets out the motion the founder runs personally, from finding the people with the pain to closing the buyer.

## When to use

- Users are active, but few or none pay.
- The founder has not sold before and avoids the conversation.
- Deals stall at "let me think about it".

Route elsewhere when:

- The team already sells and needs its process built. Use `sales-playbook-foundations`.
- The question is which channel to use for demand. Use the relevant outbound or content skill.

## Inputs

- The list of current users, with their usage, plan, and any requests they have made.
- What the product is used for, and what the users did before.
- Any conversations already held, and the outcome of each.

## Procedure

1. **Start with the users already engaged.** Look at usage to find the account with several active seats, the person who raised repeated issues, or the team that hit a limit. They have shown the pain. Do not begin with a cold list.
2. **Write to each one as a person, not a sequence.** One short message about a specific thing the team noticed. If the message could have gone to a thousand people, rewrite it.
3. **Run discovery, not a pitch.** The customer should talk most of the time. Learn what they use the product for, what they did before, what the problem costs, who else feels it, and who controls the budget.
4. **Check the four elements of a real deal** before going further:
   - Champion: uses the product and will fight for it internally.
   - Buyer: controls the budget, who is often not the champion.
   - Pain with a cost: money, time, or risk, measurable now.
   - Trigger: a reason to act in this period.
   If one is missing, the conversation is not yet a deal.
5. **Demonstrate the customer's own use case,** with their kind of data, to the outcome they want. A tour of features is not a demonstration of value.
6. **Frame value for the buyer.** The champion feels the time saved. The buyer approves budget for an outcome. Turn the first into the second.
7. **Equip the champion.** Give them a one-paragraph business case, the key number, and the answer to why now, so they can take it to the person who approves.
8. **End every conversation** with a specific next step and a date.
9. **Handle "let me think about it" as an open question.** Ask what would need to be true to move forward. The answer identifies the real blocker: budget, a stakeholder, a missing feature, or timing.

## Output

- The deals in progress, each with the four elements marked as present or missing.
- The next step and date for each conversation.
- The one-paragraph business case for each champion.
- A list of open blockers, with the question that would resolve each.

## Quality bar

Every conversation ends with a dated next step. Each deal names the buyer, not only the champion. Demonstrations show the customer's problem solved. Watch for cold outreach to lists, deals with only an enthusiastic user and no buyer, and "let me think about it" accepted without a follow-up question.
