# Creator sponsored post drafting

## Purpose

A sponsored post succeeds when it reads like a practitioner describing what worked. It fails when it reads like a press release. The difference shows in click-through and in the comments. This skill gives the structure and the checks for drafting or reviewing a post before it goes live.

## When to use

- A creator has been briefed and a draft is needed.
- A creator's draft is being reviewed before publication.
- A sponsored post has poor click-through or a flat response, and the team wants to know why.

Route elsewhere when:

- The question is the brief or the strategy. Use `creator-programme-strategy`.
- The question is choosing the creator. Use `creator-discovery-and-vetting`.
- The question is the wording of the company's own ads. Use `ad-copy-voice-of-customer`.

## Inputs

- The creator's voice and typical post format.
- The product's one measured outcome, supplied and approved by the company.
- The call to action the campaign needs.
- The disclosure the platform and the jurisdiction require.

## The four-part structure

1. **Hook (the first line).** Stop the reader with something concrete, specific, or surprising. Feeds cut off the text after a short length, so the first line must work alone. Works: a specific claim about the creator's own week. Fails: a rhetorical question, a generic claim, or a brand name as the opener.
2. **Story.** The creator's lived experience of the problem and the attempt to fix it. A good story contains four signals:
   - a specific detail, such as a time or routine,
   - a specific number from the creator's own work,
   - a failed attempt before the fix, which makes the story more credible,
   - a stake inside the creator's organisation, such as a question from their own manager.
3. **Resolution.** The product, in one or two sentences, with one measured outcome. Only the outcome the company has supplied and the creator has approved may appear.
4. **Call to action.** One clear next step. Patterns that work are a specific question to the reader, a link to a relevant resource, or an invitation to compare notes. Avoid a generic "learn more".

## Anti-patterns

An audience recognises an advertisement by these signs, so avoid them:
- an opener that names the brand,
- claims with no story behind them,
- a list of product features,
- an outcome that is too large or too neat to believe,
- a call to action that is a sales demand.

## Procedure

1. **Check the inputs:** the outcome is supplied and approved, the disclosure is in place, and the call to action is set.
2. **Draft or review the hook,** and test it by reading it alone.
3. **Check the story** for the four signals. Add any that are missing from the creator's own experience, never invented.
4. **Check the resolution** for one outcome only, and for the product stated in plain words.
5. **Check the call to action** for a single step.
6. **Scan for anti-patterns** and remove each one found.
7. **Return the draft to the creator** for their edits, and keep their final version as the approved text.

## Output

- The draft or review notes, marked by the four parts.
- The signals present and missing in the story.
- The anti-patterns found and fixed.
- The approved final text.

## Quality bar

Every outcome is supplied and approved. The hook works without the rest of the post. The story is the creator's own experience, not invented. The post has one call to action. The disclosure is present. Watch for an invented result, a brand-name opener, and a post that lists features.
