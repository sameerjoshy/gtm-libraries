# Positioning and messaging designer

## Purpose

Most companies go from ICP straight to copy. The result is inconsistent messaging, constant rewriting, and sales reps who explain the product three different ways. This skill inserts the missing step: a positioning and messaging framework that every channel draws from.

The core insight: you do not invent messaging. You extract it from the contrast between the customer's current reality and what becomes possible for them. The Use Case Messaging Canvas and the opposites method make that extraction systematic.

Positioning is evidence-bound. If the framework cannot be traced to real customer language from interviews, deal notes, or call recordings, it is a hypothesis.

## When to use

- Messaging differs across the website, outbound, sales decks, and proposals.
- The company cannot say in one sentence what makes it different.
- Copy is being written, and no positioning exists to write it from.
- The ICP has just been built, and the next step is translating it into a message.
- Sales reps give different answers to "what do you do?"

Route elsewhere when:

- The ICP itself is not yet defined or evidenced. Use `icp-builder`.
- The question is the competitive landscape and white space. Use `competitive-white-space`.
- The question is channel copy for a specific campaign. Use the relevant outreach or content skill.
- The question is the battlecard for a named competitor. Use `competitive-intelligence`.

## Inputs

- The ICP, with segments and personas, from `icp-builder`.
- Customer interview notes, discovery call notes, or deal records containing the buyer's own description of the problem. Without these, stop. The positioning would be fiction.
- Lost-deal reasons and the competitors named in them.
- Existing messaging, including website copy, decks, and outreach sequences.
- Product capabilities, with the evidence behind each benefit claimed.

If there are no customer interviews or deal notes, the first task is to collect them. Do not write positioning from the founders' description alone.

## The chain

```
ICP + evidence  →  positioning  →  messaging framework  →  copy in every channel
```

Skip a link and the downstream copy has to be rewritten. This skill covers the middle two.

## Procedure

### Step 1: Fill the twelve positioning elements

Positioning answers "why choose you over the alternative?" It is the structure that makes the solution visible in a crowded market, not a tagline. Work through the twelve elements. Every entry must come from the evidence collected above.

**Primary elements (why choose you):**
1. **Category.** The market the company plays in, in the words buyers use.
2. **Alternative.** What the buyer compares it to: a competitor, doing nothing, or building in-house.
3. **Differentiation.** What makes the company meaningfully different from that alternative.
4. **Capabilities.** What the buyer can do with this product that they cannot do otherwise.
5. **Benefits.** The results the buyer gets from those capabilities.
6. **Features.** What makes the capabilities work.

**Secondary elements (add specificity):**
7. **Personas.** Who benefits most, by role.
8. **Use case.** The job the buyer is hiring the product to do.
9. **Overarching problem.** The larger struggle the use case belongs to.
10. **Sub-problems, one to three.** The specific blockers, taken from the pain the buyers described.

Elements eleven and twelve are the supporting proof and the competitive contrast, to be written once the first ten are evidenced. Mark any element that rests on an assumption as an assumption.

### Step 2: Write one positioning line

Choose one of six formulas. Use one only.

| Formula | Structure | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Problem and outcome | "We help [who] reduce [pain] so they [result]" | The main value is removing a costly problem |
| Job to be done | "When you need to [job], use us instead of [old way]" | The job is clear and the company is the obvious alternative |
| Category and competitor | "Unlike [alternative], we [difference]" | The company competes for budget against a named rival |
| Segment | "[Category] built for [niche], not generic [everyone]" | The company owns a specific niche |
| Primary value | "The most [value] way for [who] to [result]" | The differentiator is speed, cost, or simplicity |
| Archetype | "The [archetype] in [category] for [who] who need [job]" | The company positions as a platform, not a tool |

Pick one. Make it repeatable. Back it with proof. Use it everywhere the company speaks.

### Step 3: Build the Use Case Messaging Canvas

The canvas is the core deliverable. It has two sides, the old way and the new way, joined by a use case and a persona.

**Left side: the customer's reality.** Fill this first, from interview language.
- **Overarching problem.** The big struggle, in the customer's words.
- **Three specific problems.** Pain one, two, and three, in the customer's exact language.
- **Limitation of the current way.** What is broken about how they do it now.
- **Current way.** What they actually do today, step by step.

If a line does not come from customer evidence, it does not belong on the left.

**Centre: the job and the person.**
- **Use case.** The specific job or outcome.
- **Persona.** Who in the buying group benefits, with title and context.

**Right side: the new way, built by the opposites method.** Every element on the right is the opposite of an element on the left.
- Problem becomes **benefit**: the direct resolution of the pain.
- Limitation becomes **product feature**: what was broken becomes what is now possible.
- Current way becomes **capability**: what they did by hand becomes what the product does.
- Overarching problem becomes **desired outcome**: the end state the customer wants.

The opposites write the messaging. You do not invent it. You read it off the contrast.

### Step 4: Treat the framework as the operating system

Three tools work together.

| Tool | Answers | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Customer evidence | What to say (substance) | The canvas's left side |
| Canvas | Where to put it (structure) | Copy outlines |
| Opposites | How to say it (contrast) | Every headline, email, and talk track |

The canvas feeds social posts, website copy, sales decks, email sequences, and proposal templates. Old-way and new-way contrasts tend to work well in short-form content, but test that on the client's own audience before relying on it.

### Step 5: Validate against real signals

Positioning is tested by what happens in the market, not by how the team feels about it.

**Immediate signals (weeks one and two).**
- Opening rate of outbound messages, tracked by message version.
- Discovery speed: how quickly a prospect shows they understand what the company does. Default target: about three minutes.
- Win and loss analysis: whether the company is winning against the intended competitor.

**Medium-term signals (months one to three).**
- Conversion on web pages, compared by message variant.
- Proposal iterations: fewer revisions suggests clearer messaging.
- Win rate against the named alternative.
- Whether reps repeat the positioning naturally. If they cannot, it is too complex.

**Leading indicators.**
- Time from first touch to a meaningful conversation.
- Whether prospect objections change. A good positioning removes old objections, such as "aren't you like competitor X?", and creates room for product-specific ones.
- Whether existing customers, in reference calls, use the same language.

Track each indicator against a baseline set before the change, so the effect can be seen.

### Step 6: Apply the clarity tests

- **30-second clarity test.** Read the positioning line to someone outside the company. After thirty seconds, ask them to say what the company does and for whom. If they cannot, iterate. If they can, test with five more people before accepting it.
- **Three-priority test.** Can every person in the revenue team name the top three value propositions without notes? If not, simplify.
- **Time to understanding.** Track how long it takes a prospect on a call to grasp the offer. Shorter is better, but measure it on the client's calls, not in a workshop.

### Step 7: Run the 30-day validation sprint

Do not treat the canvas as finished. Test it.
1. Week one: a batch of outbound calls or messages using the new framing, by business development representatives.
2. Week two: discovery calls by account executives using the new framing.
3. Week three: existing customers react to the new positioning, in reference calls or interviews.
4. Week four: decide whether to lock the framework or run another cycle, using the signals from Step 5.

### Step 8: Roll out to sales

Positioning is useful only if the sales team uses it consistently. Roll out in five stages.

**Readiness check first.**
- The sales leader should repeat the positioning line without notes. If they cannot, they will not enforce it.
- Play the positioning against recent lost deals. Would reps believe it would have changed the outcome? If not, it needs refining.
- Test whether a rep can use the positioning on a call without sounding scripted. If it feels unnatural, it will be ignored.

**Week one: workshop.** Hold a full-team session with sales, customer success, marketing, and product. Present the twelve elements, the chosen line, and the canvas. Replay a recorded call where the old message was muddled, using the new framing, to build belief.

**Weeks two and three: enablement.** Put every messaging artifact (line, elevator pitch, email templates, deck slides, social version) in one shared place. Run three practice sessions: a cold discovery call, handling an inbound, and handling one objection with the positioning. Review three recorded calls per rep.

**Week four: soft rollout.** Account executives use the new positioning with prospects already in active deals. Track whether prospects reach deeper stages faster. Collect feedback and adjust for one more week.

**Week five onward: full rollout.** All outbound and discovery calls use the positioning. Business development messaging aligns with account executive messaging. Add a qualification question: is this prospect aware of the alternative the positioning is built against?

**Adoption measures.**
- Message consistency: in ten random call recordings, do reps mention the core positioning? Default target eight of ten.
- Time to understanding on recorded discovery calls: does the prospect move from problem to solution within about three minutes of the framing?
- Objection shift: do the old objections fall away, and product objections replace them? Track by keyword in deal notes and calls.

**When a rep says the positioning does not work.** Ask which deal it failed on. Review the recording together. In most cases the positioning was sound, but the rep did not lead with it. The fix is practice, not a new positioning. If the customers genuinely do not share the alternative, re-check the evidence in Step 1 and test with five prospects before changing the framework.

## Common mistakes

1. **Positioning before evidence.** Guessing instead of listening. Go back to the interviews.
2. **Filling the right side first.** The company's narrative overwrites the customer's reality. Always start from the old way.
3. **Using polished words instead of customer words.** The messaging stops resonating. Use the language the customers used.
4. **Breaking the opposites.** The new way does not clearly contrast with the old way. Every right-side element should be a direct reversal.
5. **Producing a document instead of a system.** The canvas should be a working reference the team uses on calls, not a deck that gathers dust.

## Evidence and narrative checks

Before choosing a positioning, check the evidence. Ask why buyers actually switched to the company, and what stopped the ones who did not. A positioning that the switching evidence does not support is a hypothesis.

After the messaging is drafted, test the narrative. Ask what has changed in the world that makes the problem urgent now. A message with no change behind it reads as generic, however clear the wording.

## Output

- The twelve positioning elements, each marked as evidenced or assumed.
- The chosen positioning line, with the formula used.
- The Use Case Messaging Canvas, both sides, with the opposites checked.
- The validation plan, with the signals and their baselines.
- The clarity test results.
- The sales rollout plan, with the adoption measures.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Which customer sentence, from an interview or call, supports each of the three problems on the canvas?
2. Can a rep say the positioning line without notes, and can the sales leader?
3. What share of recent lost deals would the positioning have changed, and why?
4. Which two claims in the framework have no customer evidence behind them yet?
5. Which channel is still using a different message, and who owns changing it?

## Quality bar

Every element on the left of the canvas traces to a customer's words. Every right-side element is the opposite of a left-side element. The positioning line passes the thirty-second test, and sales uses it on calls within a month. Watch for positioning written without interviews, messaging that differs between channels, copy written before the framework, and a canvas nobody opens after the workshop.
