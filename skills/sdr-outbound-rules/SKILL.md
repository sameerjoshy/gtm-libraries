# SDR outbound rules

## Purpose

SDR outbound fails in predictable ways: long emails, several asks in one message, vague claims, and a corporate tone that readers skip. This skill gives every SDR the same standard, a set of frameworks to choose from, and a checklist to run before any message is sent. The rules are a floor for quality. They do not replace judgment about the prospect.

## When to use

- Training new SDRs on written outbound.
- Reviewing or rewriting drafts before they are sent.
- Setting tone for a new offer type.
- Checking whether a campaign's copy follows the team's standard.

Route elsewhere when:

- The question is whether the offer is worth making. Use `cold-offer-architect`.
- The question is the sequence and its timing. Use `outbound-cold-email`.
- The question is why a campaign is underperforming. Use `outbound-performance-diagnosis`.

## Inputs

- The prospect's name, company, role, and industry.
- The challenge and the goal, if known.
- The trigger or source: why this person, why now.
- The familiarity level: cold, previously engaged, or referred.
- The offer type, which sets the tone.

## Rules

**Length and structure**

- Sixty to ninety words, maximum.
- No bullet points in a cold email.
- One call to action. Clear, direct, respectful of time, and scheduling-oriented where possible.

**Personalization**

- Use at least one of: a trigger, a specific detail about the company, or a role-specific problem.
- Every personalization must be true and checkable. Never invent a detail to sound relevant.

**Proof**

- Social proof uses specific numbers and a real comparable, with permission where a name is used.
- Never claim a result that cannot be defended.

**Tone by offer type**

- Consultative services: calm, strategic, a trusted adviser.
- Accelerator or program: friendly, curious, approachable.
- Outbound cold: a clear pattern interrupt, short and direct, never aggressive.

### The eight frameworks

Choose one per email. Do not mix two in one message.

1. **Before and after:** the current state, then the state the reader wants.
2. **Pattern interrupt:** break the expected shape of a sales email in a way that is relevant.
3. **Ask before pitch:** open with a question that the reader can answer, then offer value.
4. **Upfront value:** give something useful before asking for anything.
5. **Do the math:** show the impact in numbers the reader can check.
6. **Comparison with similar companies:** name a pattern seen in companies like theirs.
7. **Neutral insight:** share a third-party finding, without pitching, to earn attention.
8. **Typical problems by role:** name a problem that is common for their role, and ask whether it applies.

## Procedure

1. Choose the framework from the trigger and the familiarity level.
2. Draft within the length rule, with one call to action.
3. Run the pre-send checklist:
   - Under ninety words?
   - One call to action?
   - Trigger or personalization included, and true?
   - Proof specific, with numbers where used?
   - Tone right for the offer type?
   - No buzzwords, and no fake enthusiasm?
   - Would the writer reply to this?
4. If the draft fails any item, rewrite the failed part and check again.

## Output

- The draft, with the framework named.
- The checklist result, item by item.
- For a rewrite: the original, the changes, and the reason for each.

## Quality bar

Every draft is within the length rule, has one call to action, and carries a true personalization. Proof is specific or absent. The framework is named. Watch for drafts over a hundred and fifty words, several asks in one message, and invented details.
