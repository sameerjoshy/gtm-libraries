# Ad copy from the customer's voice

## Purpose

Most B2B ad copy describes features in the product's marketing language. Buyers scroll past it because it sounds like every other vendor. Copy written from the words buyers use about their own problem is more specific and more likely to be read. This skill sets the process: gather the language first, then write.

## When to use

- A paid campaign needs headlines or body copy.
- Existing ads read like generic product marketing and are not converting.
- A new segment or persona needs its own copy.

Route elsewhere when:

- The question is who the buyer is. Use `ad-persona-framework`.
- The question is the offer the ad promotes. Use `paid-offer-strategy`.
- The question is one specific channel's format limits. Use the relevant channel skill.

## Inputs

- The persona or segment the copy is written for.
- Voice-of-customer material: reviews, discovery call notes, support tickets, and testimonials.
- The offer, and the single action the ad asks for.
- The channel's character limits, if known.

## Rule one: write from the customer's words

Brand language describes features: "real-time visibility across all business units". Customer language describes the change in daily work: "I used to spend six to eight hours a week on reporting. Now it takes fifteen minutes." Customer language wins because it mirrors what the buyer already thinks and feels.

Before writing a line of copy, gather the buyer's own language. Do not write from the product page.

## Sources, ranked

1. **Direct customer language:** reviews on software listing sites, which describe the problem before and the outcome after.
2. **Discovery call notes and transcripts:** how prospects describe the problem before they know the solution. Unfiltered and the most useful.
3. **Customer testimonials:** specific, but often edited by the vendor. Check against the original.
4. **Support and onboarding tickets:** what customers struggled with, in their own words.
5. **Product-page language:** last, because it is the brand's own language.

For each source, record the before state (what was painful), the after state (what changed), and the exact phrase.

## Headline formulas to test

Use these as starting points. Write three or more headlines per formula and test them against each other:

- A named pain in the buyer's words.
- A specific before and after, with a number the customer gave.
- A role-specific outcome for the persona.
- A question the buyer is already asking themselves.

Do not invent numbers. Use only figures a customer or the company's own data has provided.

## Procedure

1. **Collect the voice-of-customer material** from the ranked sources, and record the before state, the after state, and the exact phrasing for each.
2. **Pick the pain the segment names most often,** using the frequency in the source material.
3. **Write the headlines** from the formulas, using the customer's phrasing.
4. **Write the body** to name the change in the buyer's daily work, then the one action the offer asks for.
5. **Test one element at a time.** Change the headline, or the body, or the image, not all three, so the result can be attributed.
6. **Record each variant** with the phrase it used, so the team learns which customer language works.

## Output

- Voice-of-customer notes: before state, after state, and exact phrases, by source.
- Three or more headline variants per formula, each traceable to a source phrase.
- A body draft that names the change and the action.
- A test plan that changes one element at a time.

## Quality bar

Every headline uses phrasing traceable to a customer source. No figure appears without a source. Copy is tested one element at a time. Watch for copy written from the product page, invented customer quotes or statistics, and multiple elements changed at once so no result can be attributed.
