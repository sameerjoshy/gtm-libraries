# Multi-threading coach

## Purpose

A single-threaded deal dies when its one contact leaves, goes on leave, or loses an internal argument. Win across the buying committee, not through one person. Multi-threading is coalition-building: map who is involved, separate who you can reach (access) from who moves the decision (influence), and close the gap between them. It is not copying more people on an email.

## When to use

Run this when any of these are true:

- All engagement on the deal runs through one contact.
- You cannot say with confidence who owns the decision, the budget, or the veto.
- The champion has gone quiet and you have no other thread.
- The buying process is growing (security, procurement, finance, or an executive sponsor is now involved).
- You want a route to a high-influence person you have no access to.

Do not run this on an unqualified deal. Coalition work on a deal with no validated need produces contacts who have no reason to care.

## Inputs

Collect these before scoring. Where something is unknown, write `[CONFIRM WITH CHAMPION]` and make it a coalition task. Only the buyer knows their own organisation.

- Deal context: company, stage, value if known, and the outcome the buyer wants.
- Every stakeholder you know of: name, role, and how you know them (champion, economic buyer, users, IT or RevOps, security, legal, procurement, finance, executive sponsor).
- Your relationship with each person: spoken, met, copied only, or never contacted.
- What each person cares about, and what a "yes" requires from them.
- Known blockers or skeptics, and the reason.

If your only real input is one champion, that is the finding. Go straight to the single-thread flag in Step 3 and build the plan to widen the deal.

## Procedure

### Step 1: Map the buying committee

List everyone involved in the decision, not only the people you like talking to. For each person record:

- Role and the buyer job they own (evaluate, use, secure, approve budget, sign).
- Current stance: supportive, neutral, skeptical, blocker, or unknown.

If you know a role exists but not who holds it, write `[CONFIRM WITH CHAMPION]`. The champion supplies the name. You do not guess it.

Record each person's relationship state, because each implies a different next move:

- **Current contact with history:** still at the account, with prior exchanges.
- **Former contact still at the account:** a relationship that has gone quiet.
- **Former contact who moved:** often the highest-value hook in the map. A champion from a lost or stalled deal who now works at another target account is frequently the warmest route in.
- **Net-new, no history:** flag it. The next move should reflect that there is no relationship.

Never drop a former contact because they have left. Check where they work now. Put role gaps at the top of the map, not at the end.

### Step 2: Score access and influence

Score every contact on two separate dimensions. Use High, Medium, or Low. Be honest. Inflated access scores are how reps mistake a dead deal for a live one.

- **Access:** how directly and reliably you can reach this person.
  - High: a real two-way relationship.
  - Medium: you have been introduced, or the champion can get you there.
  - Low: you know they exist but have no path yet.
- **Influence:** how much this person moves this decision.
  - High: decides, funds, or holds a veto.
  - Medium: shapes the outcome or gates one step.
  - Low: consulted, but does not move it.

### Step 3: Plot the grid and flag single-thread risk

Place each contact on the access by influence grid:

- **High access, high influence:** your backers. Protect them and equip them to sell internally.
- **Low access, high influence:** the priority gap. These people decide and you cannot reliably reach them. Closing this gap is the core of the plan.
- **High access, low influence:** useful for intelligence and introductions. They cannot close the deal alone. Do not count their enthusiasm as progress.
- **Low access, low influence:** deprioritise.

Flag single-thread risk explicitly when every high-access contact is one person, or when every high-influence person sits in the low-access column. Name the consequence: "If [champion] goes quiet, we have no thread to the economic buyer."

### Step 4: Build the coalition plan

For each stakeholder worth engaging, write one concrete move.

- **Low-access, high-influence contacts:** the route to reach them. Prefer a champion-led introduction over cold outreach. Give the champion a reason that helps them, not only you. Example: "Would you introduce me to [name] so I can address the security review directly and save your team a round of questions?"
- **Each thread:** one or two lines of talking point, framed around what that person cares about. Security cares about risk and review burden. Finance cares about return and scenarios. The executive sponsor cares about the strategic outcome. Users care about their day getting easier. Do not reuse one pitch for everyone.
- **Backers:** give high-access, high-influence champions the case to sell internally. Do not just lean on them.
- **Sequence:** the order to open threads, and which threads depend on an introduction landing first.

Routing rules:

- With an engaged champion, default to the introduction route. Contacting the economic buyer directly, bypassing the champion, can damage a late-stage deal.
- Do not suggest a contact who has been engaged in the last 30 days. Check the engagement record first.
- If the deal is single-threaded and the one contact has gone silent, that is a stalled-deal problem, not a threading problem. Route it to stalled-deal recovery rather than this skill.

Never invent a stakeholder's name, a priority, or a relationship you do not have. A plan built on imagined access convinces a rep that a dead deal is alive.

Every new thread must have a reason to exist and something in it for that person. Coalition work is not blast outreach. Outreach to any new contact goes to the approver first, under the library's rule that no external message is sent without human approval.

### Step 5: Set the re-check cadence

Access and influence move as the deal moves. New people arrive, sentiment shifts, a champion is promoted or sidelined. Re-map at each stage gate, and whenever a new stakeholder appears. Otherwise the map freezes at first contact.

## Output

One coalition plan, containing:

- **Committee map:** everyone involved, with role, buyer job, and stance.
- **Scores:** access and influence for each contact, honestly rated.
- **Grid:** contacts placed on it, with the low-access, high-influence quadrant called out.
- **Single-thread flag:** stated explicitly, with the consequence if the current thread breaks.
- **Coalition moves:** one per stakeholder worth engaging, including the introduction path for each high-influence gap and a talking point per thread.
- **Confirm list:** every `[CONFIRM WITH CHAMPION]` item as a coalition task.
- **Re-check date:** when the map is next refreshed.

## Quality bar

A strong plan makes the deal survive the loss of any one person. There are at least two live threads into the decision. The economic buyer is reachable, or there is a concrete route in motion to reach them. Each thread has a reason that serves the other person.

Signs it is working:

- More than one high-access thread, and a route to every high-influence person.
- The champion makes introductions because the plan gave them a reason that serves them.
- Talking points differ by persona.
- Single-thread risk was named early and fixed, not discovered at close.

Signs it is off track:

- Every access score is High. The rep is grading themselves.
- The plan is "add more people to the thread" with no reason or talking point for each.
- High-influence people sit in the low-access column with no route to them.
- Low-influence enthusiasm is being counted as deal progress.
